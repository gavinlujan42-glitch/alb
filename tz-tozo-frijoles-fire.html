TZ // TOZO // ROAD NOTES 002 // Española–Santa Fe Corridor // August 20, 2026

REPORTING
FROM HELL
ON EARTH

This morning La Bajada opened onto a Santa Fe swallowed by smoke. The sun was in the windshield. The mountains were ghosts. Up the road, the Frijoles Fire kept writing its own weather.

REAL-WORLD FIRE CONTEXT: The lightning-caused Frijoles Fire began August 4. Official Forest Service updates reported 11,335 acres and 9% containment on August 17; a community report on August 19 listed 12,974 acres and 31% containment. Fire operations and closures are active in the area. This column does not approach or enter closure zones. Never travel toward an active wildfire for sightseeing or reporting without authorization.

This morning, coming into Santa Fe, it seemed for a minute like I had driven into somebody else's nightmare.

You know that place at the top of La Bajada where the road crests and the whole world suddenly opens? And this time of year the sun sits right there in the windshield, a white-hot coin straight in your eyes, everybody squinting and braking and hoping the person behind them is paying attention.

Usually you crest that hill and Santa Fe is laid out ahead like an old postcard somebody left in the sun.

Not this morning.

Smoke.

Smoke across the basin. Smoke eating the edges of the mountains. Smoke turning distance into layers of brown and gray. The early-morning air had pressed it down low, and from La Bajada the city looked less like the City Different and more like a city under siege.

Maybe that's too dramatic. TOZO is allowed a little drama. But wildfire smoke does that to a place. Familiar geography becomes foreign. The Sangres disappear. The horizon gets erased. Daylight looks counterfeit.

Santa Fe looked like a city at war. Perhaps, in the old human sense, it was: people against fire, crews against terrain, communities against the possibility that the wind might choose them next.

The fire up the road

The Frijoles Fire started with lightning on August 4 and pushed into hard country in and around the Pecos Wilderness. Firefighters have been building direct and contingency lines while communities including Cundiyó, Cerro Piñon and areas around Nambé have lived with Ready, Set or Go notices as conditions changed.

So TOZO is not going charging through a roadblock with a notebook yelling about freedom of the press. Nel. The closest useful reporting point is the closest legal and safe point. Firefighters have enough problems without some wandering desert correspondent becoming another one.

From Española, though, you can feel the thing without seeing flame. Smoke settles into valleys. People check phones. Somebody's cousin knows somebody on a crew. Somebody has livestock. Somebody has an elderly parent. Somebody remembers Cerro Grande, Las Conchas, Hermits Peak. Fire in northern New Mexico arrives with ancestors.

Then the phone rang

I made it to work.

Stayed maybe an hour and a half.

Then came the call: Mom is in the ER.

And suddenly the enormous fire on the horizon became background.

I left.

No committee meeting. No email. No calendar block. No professional theater about being indispensable. There are moments when the hierarchy of the universe becomes extremely clear, extremely fast.

Work matters. Responsibility matters. Showing up matters.

But when your mother is in the emergency room, you go.

Priorities are easy to describe when nothing is happening. You find out what they really are when the phone rings.

So today's dispatch doesn't end at the fire line. It ends in fluorescent hospital light, with smoke still sitting over northern New Mexico and hundreds of firefighters somewhere up the road doing their own version of exactly the same thing: going where they are needed.

Maybe that's the signal today.

The landscape burns. The calendar burns. Plans burn.

And you decide what does not.

Nos vemos down the road.
