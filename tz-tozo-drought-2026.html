TZ TOZO // Road Notes 003 // August 2026 // Somewhere the ditch is running low

THE RIVER IS
SENDING A MESSAGE

Global warming doesn't exist. Climate change doesn't exist. And if you believe that, homes, I have an acequia full of water to sell you.

TOZO here. Maybe Española. Maybe Burque. Maybe sitting under a cottonwood watching a ribbon of Rio Grande move through a channel that remembers being bigger.

There is a particular Southwestern talent for arguing with the thermometer while standing in the heat. The land, meanwhile, does not debate. It just keeps the books.

THE BOOKS, AUGUST 2026: Drought.gov reports about 1.9 million New Mexicans living in drought areas. July was the state's 29th driest on record since 1895, and January through July ranked 23rd driest. The Rio Grande watershed drought dashboard estimates hundreds of thousands of acres of hay, sorghum and cotton, plus large livestock inventories, are in drought areas.

Last winter's record-low snowpack matters because snow is the mountain bank account. Melt it too early, receive too little, and summer arrives looking for withdrawals from an account that never got funded. Federal drought analysts warned in June that even a decent monsoon would do little to repair long-term Rio Grande streamflow or reservoir storage.

Water Has A Price Before It Has A Price Tag

When surface water shrinks, agriculture feels it early. Irrigation gets harder. Wells become more tempting and more expensive. Pasture suffers. Feed costs move. Crop choices change. Some acres simply do not get planted.

Then the consequence walks downstream. Less local production can mean tighter margins, higher costs and more dependence on food grown somewhere else, somewhere that may be dealing with its own heat and water problems.

The chile does not care which cable-news channel you watch. It wants water.

Warmer air also pulls more moisture from soil and vegetation through evapotranspiration. Drought.gov explicitly notes that higher temperatures increase evapotranspiration and reduce soil moisture. Dry vegetation then becomes better wildfire fuel. We have already been breathing that equation.

So Guess What Comes Next?

Not apocalypse tomorrow morning. That's lazy prophecy. The serious future is more complicated: competition.

Farm versus city. Surface water versus groundwater. Growth versus supply. Senior rights versus junior rights. Ecosystem needs versus diversion. Cheap food versus expensive inputs. Development plans versus the physical amount of wet stuff actually available.

And that means New Mexico's next great infrastructure project is not merely another dam, pipe or database. It is water intelligence: knowing what exists, who has rights to it, where it is moving, what is being depleted, what agriculture needs, and how decisions today propagate twenty years downstream.

We should be able to look at a basin and see the whole living system: snowpack, reservoirs, rivers, acequias, wells, rights, crops, municipal demand, drought, fire and forecast. Not as separate bureaucratic files. As one landscape.

The Old People Already Knew

Acequia culture did not begin with an assumption of infinite supply. It began with scarcity, community and obligation. You clean the ditch together because everybody downstream exists. You do not own the river merely because it passes your gate.

Maybe the future is not entirely futuristic.

Maybe some of it is remembering.

The river is sending a message. The reservoir is sending one. The farmer staring at an irrigation schedule is sending one. The smoke over the mountains is sending one.

We can argue about terminology.

The landscape has already moved on to consequences.
